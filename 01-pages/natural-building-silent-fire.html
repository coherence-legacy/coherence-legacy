huerfano county, colorado

natural building at silent fire ranch

We seek to live our lives as “creators” not “consumers.”

We have come to Huerfano County as a family and community of builders and gardeners to create a viable example of sustainable, resilient living, utilizing principles of permaculture, natural building, and off-the-grid living technologies.

As designers we have also come to Huerfano County to evolve the outward expression of off-the-grid living, permaculture, and natural building to a level of coherent design that can help further these values toward becoming a mainstream approach to life in the 21st century.

We are also focused on new economic models of local cooperation: barter, local currency, and reuse and recycling. We seek to live our lives as “creators” not “consumers.” We hope that our endeavors are putting something back rather than consuming ever more precious and depleting resources and sending our community’s valuable resources and money to corporations outside of our area, or even country, never to return to Huerfano County.

A significant criterion in our endeavor is to live debt-free. To do this we will have to spend large amounts of cash that we do not and probably will not have, or we can use our own sweat “ownership” not “equity” (a debt word) and materials that are on hand, to construct our homes and outbuildings. This will have to take place over a period of years, prioritizing our needs but working to a Master Plan.

We intend to construct two homes at Silent Fire Ranch: one single-family residence, and a “community house” for our kids, interns, and guests. The community house will be the larger of the two homes and the more intensively used of the two. This will be constructed using the lowest possible cost building system that we are aware of: Earthbag. Using this system of construction we are particularly looking for ways to cut any extraneous cost and materials that are not on hand out of the process.

To every extent possible, it is our desire to explore alternative ways of construction on our property at Majors Ranch that will enable us to build “local” to the land on which the structures are located.

natural building

With this “Local” approach to the built environment, we are here to help usher in a new/old, better way to live in the 21st century.....local.....our commitment is to build to every extent possible at Silent Fire Ranch with what we have here on site or within a 50-mile radius.

With forty years in the design and construction of world-class estates, retreat centers, and healing environments, we have come to understand how our aesthetic, values, economics, and lives have become a reflection of corporate values and ever-increasing greed.

The very “Green” ratings such as “LEED” and “Build It Green” have been designed and controlled by corporate interests to further make us consumers of their products..........and guess what......there is nothing green about materials shipped from afar, made from chemicals or from destructive use of our natural resources..........

In the construction of our own home and environment we are seeking the simplest and most profound expression of the naturalness of this beautiful land. We are seeking built space that supports us and our endeavors, rather than us supporting the built environment.

loose fit – long life – low energy

We have been designing and building homes, estates, and retreat centers for forty years throughout California and now in Colorado. Over time, we have come to understand that very clear patterns of truth were emerging in all of my projects. In time, we have adopted the principles of Loose Fit, Long Life, and Low Energy: These are an overriding set of principles that we apply to every aspect of design and planning. We believe their application is valid, not only in the process of building a dream home, healing environment, or intentional community but also in keeping it and the dream of your lifestyle alive and well. These principles will help you understand and define your grandest motivations all the way down to deciding on the smallest details involved in finishing it, making it sustainable, and keeping it all working—simply, gracefully, beautifully:

loose fit

Recognize that life is always in a state of flux. Anticipate that change is inevitable. Design and plan for adaptability. Seek to simplify wherever possible to provide for flexibility and freedom.

long life

Know thyself.......take the time to develop and understand your Nature to ensure the longevity of your creation. Design and build with natural materials that are healthful and have always stood the test of time. Use simple, time-tested systems that are low cost and reliable. Do not add to the body of kitsch. It has always been generated side by side with the natural evolution of the classic traditions that mark what endures. Design and build for generations to come with a clear understanding that tradition is not a relic of the past, that it is a living force that animates and informs the present. Tradition is not repetition of what has been, but rather it anticipates the reality of what endures, and as such, tradition is carried forward to produce something new.

low energy

Save resources and energy. In our lifetimes, the world population will double and natural resources are diminishing at a corresponding rate. Invest now for maximum efficiency and design to provide for future adaptability to new technology. Do not spend too much. Making money already takes a huge amount of energy. Freedom is the difference between what you make and what you spend. Freedom is quality of life. Make sure that the home you create is life-sustaining and adds to your enjoyment rather than detracts from your quality of life with excessive fussiness and maintenance.

wabi sabi

We live in a very engaging, fast-paced world full of wonderful stimulation, involvements, and distractions that we are actively engaged at many levels. Yet as we have specialized in country homes, estates, retreats, and healing centers we are usually creating sanctuaries designed for peaceful, beautiful, enlightened, magical lifestyles. A guiding light for us in this endeavor is a small book called Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, and Philosophers. Over the years, as we have worked with this aesthetic code that informs the Zen traditions of Japanese design, we have found that the way of wabi-sabi is just as relevant to what we love about almost all indigenous, authentic, and weathering beauty of every other culture on the earth.

We are designers not writers therefore much of what is below are the words of the author of wabi-sabi, Leonard Koren. We could not better articulate a set of values that reverberate to the essence of my design conviction. With the inspiration of wabi-sabi it is possible to accomplish the following:

  • Create an environment where one “…can pay maximum attention to everything happening at this very moment:” an environment in which “to be here now”.
  • It is the celebration of the fullness and balance of life that is the underlying purpose of a home, a healing retreat, or community. “…Combine the skills of architecture, interior and landscape design, art, food preparation and service that coalesces into a quietly exciting event that thematically coheres.”
  • Learn to see “…Slow way down, be patient and look very closely.”
  • Assist all who come to Silent Fire Ranch to experience an environment of beauty at every level: “Wabi-Sabi suggests that beauty is a dynamic event that occurs between you and something else...... Beauty can spontaneously occur at any moment given the proper circumstances, context, or point of view. Beauty is thus an altered state of consciousness, an extraordinary moment of poetry and grace.”
  • Assist all who come to Silent Fire Ranch to place our materialism in perspective: “Even at the most austere level of material existence, we still live in a world of things. Wabi-Sabi is exactly about the delicate balance between the pleasure we get from things and the pleasure we get from the freedom from things.”
  • Celebrate naturalness: “Things wabi-sabi are expressions of time frozen. They are made of materials that are visibly vulnerable to the effects of weathering and human treatment. They record the sun, wind, rain, heat, and cold in a language of discoloration, rust, tarnish, stain, warping, shrinking, shriveling, and cracking. Their nicks, chips, bruises, scars, dents, peeling and other forms of attrition are a testament to histories of use and misuse. Though things wabi-sabi may be at the point of dematerialization (or materialization), extremely faint, fragile or desiccated they still possess an undiminished poise and strength of character.”
  • “Things wabi-sabi are unstudied and inevitable looking. They do not blare out ‘I am important’ or demand to be the center of attention. They are understated and unassuming, yet not without presence or quiet authority. Things wabi-sabi easily coexist with the rest of their environment.”
  • “Simplicity is at the core of things wabi-sabi. The simplicity of wabi-sabi is probably best described as the state of grace arrived at by a sober, modest, heartfelt intelligence. The main strategy of this intelligence is economy of means. Pare down to the essence, but don’t remove the poetry. Keep things clean and unencumbered, but don’t sterilize.”

building debt free

  • Pay cash for the land leaves little money for construction.
  • Infrastructure is very expensive. It must be added as we can afford it.
  • Live/camp on the land as long as necessary to build without debt.
    • Camping simply means living without utilities built into the land.
    • We are camping in our two trailers even though we have self-contained fully functioning bathrooms and kitchens.
    • We bring in water with a 300-gallon truck-mounted tank.
    • We move our waste with a 200-gallon honey wagon to the SL septic tank.
    • We generate electricity with a gas and diesel-powered generator.
  • We need to be able to build support structures before we build traditional homes. Those include: barn workshop, office/studios, intern casitas, greenhouses, garden structures, chicken and livestock structures, meeting hall, indoor/outdoor kitchen.
  • Build structures first that support kids and interns who will actually be learning natural building as they construct the various systems and structures on the land.
  • Be able to build small houses as small as 200 sq. ft. initially that can be added onto over time.
  • Eliminate expensive, complex systems wherever possible.
  • Use low-cost, natural, time-tested methods for meeting needs. These will be mostly the ways we did things 150 years ago.
  • We are currently allowed to construct buildings of 120 sq. ft. without a permit. We would like to be able to construct buildings a minimum of 168’ (12 × 14’) × 15’ high that includes a loft for sleeping or storage depending on the function. These buildings will be of a similar design to create a “village”-like aesthetic. Heated by kiva fireplaces.
  • Construct assemblies of two or three of these “casita” structures that can be integrated into a larger residential structure at a later date.
  • Electricity will be provided by small localized solar systems in each structure sized to meet the needs of the purpose of that structure: painting studio, jewelry studio, office, intern quarters, etc.
  • Are we allowed to provide a “bunk house” for seasonal workers with bathroom, but no kitchen, not a home?
  • Use standing dead logs for structural floors and ceiling/roof beams? Will it have to be grade stamped?
  • We would like to keep structural engineering to a minimum. All structures will be over-constructed in general. Like familiar wood construction, we would like to be able to use conventions for building specs, and good judgment when a simple variation. Major structures like large multi-story barns and two- or three-story residences will be engineered.

“local” construction

  • Rubble or scoria trench foundations from gravel generated from screening to ¼” of the site soils for use in adobe, earthbag, rammed-earth, or sousei construction. Scoria from local quarries can also be used. Down to frost line 42” at 7,000’. Insulated both sides. If necessary pour a 12” H reinforced concrete footing on top of rubble footing.
  • Radon mitigation using on-site gravel generated from soil screening: 6” perforated flexible pipe pinned to ground with 12” spikes; 6” × ¾” drain rock filled to top.
  • Insulated floor: 2” rigid foam (is this required?), hemp (grown on site), possibly mixed with concrete.
  • Recycle as much as possible: windows, doors, old pipes, plumbing fixtures.
  • Gunite Earth Insulation/Plaster (GEP): machine applied, hand troweled; water or sand blasted to bring out rustic sandstone boulder/cliff; exterior wall contain hemp grown on site, or Styrofoam “rice.” Get clear on U-value of earth walls vs. R-Value.
  • Adobe floors over insulation and radon system, sealed to create a hard-wearing system.
  • Site-built doors and windows from local timber harvested from standing dead trees; dual glazed from local glass contractor.
  • Steel may also be considered “local” here as we have a steel mill in Pueblo. We also have recycled steel resources within a fifty-mile radius. Train car recycler is located in Alamosa.
  • Green/sod/earth roofs will provide insulation, longevity, and a very low profile to buildings.
  • Unfortunately the following materials will have to come from non-natural, non-local sources: electrical system materials, plumbing system materials, waterproofing membranes and sealants, Portland cement, PV and solar thermal materials (these can be acquired second hand locally with resources in Trinidad and Crestone). earthbag construction

Earthbag building (sometimes called sandbag building) is rapidly gaining in popularity as people discover the benefits. It’s one of the simplest, lowest-cost building systems in the world. Very few tools and almost no special skills are required. It boils down to filling bags with soil or gravel and tamping the bags solid. What could be simpler?

The military have been building bullet- and blast-resistant structures with sandbags for about a century. Lately, homeowners and professional builders have constructed homes, clinics, schools, orphanages, shops, and other structures this way.

Earthbag buildings are safe, quiet, nontoxic, rodent-proof, and fire-, hurricane-, and flood-resistant. They can be designed for any type of climate simply by changing the fill material in the bags — soil or gravel for hot climates, insulated material such as volcanic rock or vermiculite for cold or hot climates.

Earthbags are very adaptable and so you can build almost any style or shape of building imaginable: domes, vaults, structures with vertical walls, curved “organic” designs, roundhouses, you name it. In addition, you can build planters, benches, privacy and retaining walls, to name but a few.

Because polypropylene bags are designed for flood control and not affected by moisture, you can build underground structures such as earth-sheltered homes, storm cellars, and root cellars.

Earthbag buildings are sustainable since they are made primarily of earth or aggregate from or near the building site. Earthbag domes do not require wood, thereby reducing pressure on our forests. Expensive concrete foundations are not required if you use gravel-filled bags.

  • Continuous earthbag construction, long continuous bags. Use native soils with 10% clay combined with 6% Portland cement tamped flat on top.
  • Barbed wire between earthbag courses to tie courses together and provide seismic ties at every course.
  • Domed construction, no roof construction necessary; the walls become the roof.
  • We will add tie-wires through the bags to the barbed wire to tie stucco wire to the dome inside and out as an anchor for the GEP.

Additional information: earthbagbuilding.com · structure1.com · greenhomebuilding.com

Gravel foundations. Curvilinear forms. Groupings of structures. Varied aesthetics. While we love these “native” forms, we intend through all of our work to find ways to make these emerging technologies aesthetically appropriate to the mainstream market.

pueblo adobe construction

The classic pueblo-style adobe construction is of course uniquely suited to our environment. Throughout the world a language of the simplest kind of structures that provided shelter and security has evolved over thousands of years. For us this language is at the heart of an aesthetic of simplicity and authenticity to place.

Some of the principles that enable the simplest form of construction, with the lowest cost, use of natural resources along with ease of construction include:

  • Rectilinear forms
  • Flat roofs
  • No exposed wood (more fireproof)
  • No concrete used in the construction
  • Thermal mass to passively heat and cool winter and summer
  • Indoor and outdoor kitchens

Newer evolutions we see in the design idiom that will make the Pueblo style more livable and dynamic for our 21st-century lives:

  • Green roofs
  • Water catchment off roofs
  • Interior interconnection between rooms, creating a more open and flowing living environment
  • Higher ceilings
  • Large glass openings
  • French doors
  • Connection between the interior spaces and exterior living space
  • Transitional spaces such as covered entries, trellises, terraces

Indigenous architecture throughout the world utilizes similar forms. We will be combining the language of earth/adobe construction with rock columns, plinths, and terraces to solidify the expression of the earth construction and vastly increase its longevity by providing hard surfaces where wind and rain, freezing and thawing usually wear adobe structures.

Pueblo means town. This is an architecture that creates a village-like environment, a self-sustaining settlement. For us this Pueblo environment takes place in the context of agriculture and of meeting the land on its terms.

GEP (gunite earth plaster)

We seek to establish an insulation and finish system that will be possible to apply in a day. Our intention is to develop a singular approach to insulation and finish utilizing cement and the soils from the construction site, provided they do not contain too much organic matter. We imagine an insulation layer that is shotcrete or gunite applied which contains, preferably hemp, or rice-size Styrofoam to create an appropriate insulation level. Thickness of insulation coat of GEP will be determined by required R-factor combined with the U-factor of the earth construction. Then over that first level of insulating GEP, and perhaps at the same time, we will apply a topping/finish layer of GEP say ½" thick of the same materials without any insulation additives. This finish coat could be troweled smooth, or be sand-blasted while still wet, or be sand-blasted or water-blasted dry once the GEP has set up.

Natural sandstone-like surface. Carved Earth Plaster.

hemp

Hemp is an herbaceous annual that develops a rigid woody stem ranging in height from 1 to over 5 meters (3 to 19 feet). Hemp stalks have a woody core surrounded by a bark layer containing long fibers that extend nearly the entire length of the stem. Plant breeders have developed hemp varieties with increased stem fiber content and very low levels of delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the psychoactive ingredient of marijuana. THC content is about 0.3%.

Hemp grown at site for insulation and fiber. Gunite application.

Climate zones: Hemp is well adapted to the temperate zone and will grow under a wide range of environmental conditions. Hemp grows best when mean daily temperatures are between 13° and 22° C (60°–80° F), but will endure colder and warmer conditions. Hemp grows quite well at relatively low temperatures, and young seedlings will tolerate some exposure to frost. Because of this, hemp can be planted earlier than corn in most areas and will develop a closed canopy early in the season allowing increased light interception and rapid growth. The best fiber-producing hemp varieties require about 4 months without killing frost to produce fiber and about 5.5 months to mature seed.

Water requirements: Hemp requires abundant moisture throughout the growing season, particularly while young plants are becoming established during the first six weeks of growth. After they are well rooted, plants can endure drier conditions; however, severe drought hastens maturity and produces dwarfed plants. Studies in Europe indicate that hemp requires 500–700 millimeters (20–28 inches) of available moisture for optimum yield, and that 250–300 millimeters (10–14 inches) of moisture should be available during the vegetative growth stage. These amounts include both precipitation and available soil moisture. In Europe hemp yield is strongly dependent on the amount of rainfall during June and July. Crop water use will, of course, vary depending on local soil, climatic, and cultural conditions.

Current Colorado laws regarding industrial hemp are currently being reformed/evaluated. The new bill that may pass will tell us what the regulations are. We are expecting to have a definitive answer by March or April.

rammed earth greenhouses

We believe that the most flexible system to create year-round greenhouses is utilizing rammed earth directly from the site with an admixture of cement and Kalmatron or similar. A flexible set of rectilinear and curved forms can enable numerous varieties of shapes and sizes.

We imagine an active solar thermal system providing hot water to 55-gallon drums through a system of heat exchangers in each drum enabling the storage of more heat during our 300 days of sun per year. The roof and glazed sidewalls will be constructed of simple tubular steel frames welded in place on site. 5-ply polycarbonate panels will be used for glazing.
